How Soul Layers paints

These pictures are not drawn by hand. A short program throws down thousands of brush strokes, and one number, the golden ratio φ ≈ 1.618, decides where everything goes. This page walks through how it works, step by step. You don't need any maths or coding background.

1 · The big picture

One number in, one painting out

Every painting starts from a seed: a whole number like 11 or 23. The seed drives the random choices (where a stroke starts, how a mask wobbles), so the same seed always gives the same painting, and a new seed gives a new one.

2 · The golden ratio

Cut off a square, and what's left has the same shape

The golden ratio is φ = (1 + √5) / 2 ≈ 1.618. A rectangle whose long side is φ times its short side has a special property: cut a square off one end, and the leftover rectangle is a smaller copy of the original. Do it again, and again, and the squares spiral inward toward one point.

Every canvas here is such a rectangle, 1 wide and φ tall. The program cuts 14 squares (top, right, bottom, left, repeat), draws quarter-circles through them to get the golden spiral, and finds the point the squares shrink toward. We call it the pole. The pole is where the eye goes first, so that's where the light burst is painted.

Where else φ shows up in the code

The pole is only the start. Most sizes and strengths in the painting are powers of φ, so they all share one rhythm:

  • Vortex strengths are 1, 1/φ and 1/φ², so the swirl at the pole is the strongest and the others fade in golden steps.
  • Glass spheres along the spiral each have a radius 1/φ times the one before.
  • The spine in Light Cage shrinks by φ−1/8 per vertebra, so every 8 vertebrae the size drops by exactly φ.
  • Ribs get shorter by powers of φ from the middle outward.
  • Mirroring flips the layout upside down (y → φ − y). The pole moves from 27.6% to 72.4% of the height: chest level for the ribcage, and room below for the masks to drip.
pole ≈ (0.276, 0.447) on a 1 × φ canvas mirrored: 1 − 0.276 = 72.4% of the height
3 · The golden angle

Why sunflower seeds never line up

The dotted patches in the paintings are placed the way a sunflower places its seeds. Dot number n sits a little further out than dot n − 1, and is turned by a fixed angle. If that angle is a simple fraction of a full turn (like 90° or 120°), the dots fall into straight spokes with gaps between them. The golden angle, 360°/φ² ≈ 137.5°, is the angle that is "least like a fraction", so the dots never line up and fill the disc evenly.

The formula

rn = R · √(n / N) distance from the centre θn = n · 137.5° angle

The square root keeps the density even: the disc's area grows with r², so r has to grow with √n. In Soul Layers there are two of these patches, with 520 and 300 dots. Their centres sit where the golden division lines cross, at (0.618, 0.786 φ) and (0.786, 0.236 φ).

4 · Brush strokes

Every stroke follows an invisible current

Imagine the canvas covered in tiny arrows, like wind on a weather map. That's a flow field. A brush stroke starts at a random point, takes a small step in the direction of the arrow under it, looks again, takes another step, and so on. Thousands of strokes following the same arrows give the painting its sense of movement.

The arrows come from two ingredients added together: smooth waves made of sines and cosines (the gentle currents), and three vortices on the golden spiral (the whirlpools). The strongest vortex sits on the pole.

The recipe, in four lines

1. Bend the coordinates with sine waves, so straight lines become wobbly:

x̃ = x + 0.22 sin(3.3 y + p₁) ỹ = y + 0.22 sin(2.9 x + p₂)

2. Mix three waves into one number n and turn it into a direction:

n = sin(4.1 x̃ + p₃) + cos(3.4 ỹ − p₁) + 0.6 sin(8.5 (x̃ − 0.7 ỹ) + p₂) direction = (cos 1.4n, sin 1.4n)

3. Add a pull around each vortex that fades with distance. Strengths are 1, 1/φ, 1/φ² and they spin +, −, +.

4. Step the brush a fixed small distance along the result. Repeat.

The phases p₁, p₂, p₃ come from the seed. That's why a new seed gives new currents but keeps the golden layout.

5 · Three modes

Same skeleton, three different paintings

All three modes share the golden layout, the flow field and the finishing step. They differ in what they paint on top. The second image in each pair is the same painting with the golden guide switched on, so you can see the squares, the spiral and the pole underneath.

Soul Layers

Flowing strokes, overlapping glass spheres, a light burst at the pole, sunflower dots and four-pointed stars. Default palette: Kozmik.

φ: burst at the pole · vortices 1, 1/φ, 1/φ² · sphere radii ÷ φ each step · stars sized by powers of φ

Light Cage

A cobalt ribcage and spine, a yellow-magenta neon halo, a gold-white burst in the chest, DNA-like helices and sharp stars. Default palette: NeonGece (neon night).

φ: mirrored layout, pole at 72.4% · vertebrae × φ−1/8 · ribs shorten by powers of φ · rays at multiples of the golden angle

Melting Masks

A misty pastel ground with aubergine swirls at the edges, three to five green and turquoise ghost masks (one dark purple with a single blue eye), drips running down, and an oil-paint smear. Default palette: PastelRuh (pastel soul).

φ: biggest mask on the pole, others on the spiral, each radius ÷ φ · edge vortices at 0.382 / 0.618 of each side

Any palette works with any mode. These images were made with the project's Python port; the Mathematica version follows the same rules but uses a different random number generator, so the same seed won't give a pixel-identical picture.

6 · The glow

Bright parts leak light

The last step makes the painting look lit from inside. The program makes a blurred copy of the image and combines it with the original using a screen blend, the same mode photo editors use for light leaks. Where the picture is already bright, the blur adds a halo; where it's dark, almost nothing changes. Then it adds a little film grain.

I = 1 − (1 − a)(1 − g·b) a = the image b = its blurred copy g = glow amount (0.3, Light Cage 0.4, Masks 0.2)

Melting Masks gets one extra step, smear: pixels are pushed a few pixels along a slow random field, then blurred vertically, like wet oil paint dragged downward.

7 · Chaos and form

A dot that jumps a million times

The second file in the project, kaos_ve_form.wl ("chaos and form"), is a gallery of shapes that come straight out of equations. The star of it is the Clifford attractor. Start a dot at (0.1, 0.1) and move it with this rule, over and over:

xnew = sin(a·y) + c·cos(a·x) ynew = sin(b·x) + d·cos(b·y)

The dot never settles and never repeats, but it also never leaves a certain region. It keeps returning to the same invisible shape. The program counts how many times the dot lands in each pixel. Busy pixels get bright colours, rarely visited ones stay dark. A logarithm keeps the busiest spots from drowning out the faint threads. Four numbers, a, b, c, d, decide the whole shape.

This runs right in your browser with 400,000 jumps (the real images use 3 million). Drag a slider and the shape morphs. Some settings collapse to a few dots: try setting a to 0. The Random button skips those, just like the studio in the code does.

8 · A bug, explained

Why the morphing animation went dark

One animation morphs art1 into art2 and back. The first version simply slid the four numbers from art1's values to art2's. About 24 of the 60 frames came out black.

The reason is a hidden rule. Flipping the signs of some parameters gives the same shape (just mirrored), and none of those flips changes the sign of a × b. In art1, a × b is negative. In art2 it's positive. So any path between them has to cross a line where a = 0 or b = 0. On that line one of the equations stops depending on the dot's position, and the whole attractor collapses to a handful of points.

The fix: morph the dots, not the numbers

  1. Compute both attractors once: 800,000 dots each.
  2. Scale each cloud to the same square, from −1 to 1.
  3. Sort each cloud by distance from its centre, so dot #1 is the innermost and the last dot is the outermost.
  4. Pair dot #k of art1 with dot #k of art2. Each frame draws every dot part of the way between its two partners.

Inner dots flow to inner dots, outer to outer. No frame collapses, frame 0 is exactly art1 and the middle frame is exactly art2. In the code this is cliffordBulut (the sorted cloud) and yogunlukResmi (the density picture).

9 · More shapes from equations

The complex plane and the gyroid

Colouring a complex function

Every point of the picture is a complex number z. The program computes f(z) = (z⁵ − 1)/(z² + 0.3i) + sin(1/z) and paints the direction of the answer as a hue (red, yellow, green …) and its size as rings of brightness. The rainbow pinwheels are where f(z) = 0. The busy knot in the middle comes from sin(1/z), which wiggles infinitely fast near zero. The animation replaces z with z·eit, which turns the input slowly.

The gyroid

All the points in space where sin x cos y + sin y cos z + sin z cos x = 0 form one endless surface that never crosses itself. It splits space into two interlocking tunnel systems. Nature uses the same shape in the nanostructures that colour some butterfly wings. Here it is cut down to a ball. In the "breathing" animation the 0 on the right-hand side moves up and down, so the tunnels widen and narrow.

10 · Run it yourself

Two ways to make your own

With Python (no Mathematica needed)

git clone https://github.com/beriltatli/mathematica-sanat
cd mathematica-sanat
python3 -m venv .venv
.venv/bin/pip install -r python/requirements.txt
.venv/bin/python python/studyo.py        # sliders for the three painting modes
.venv/bin/python python/studyo_kaos.py   # sliders for the Clifford attractor

The studio windows have the same controls as the Mathematica version: mode, seed, palette, vortex strength, density and the golden guide. "Save" writes a 3000 px PNG, enough for a print of about 25 × 40 cm at 300 dpi.

With Mathematica or the free Wolfram Engine

Open ruh_katmanlari.wl in Mathematica and choose Run All Code. Or install the free Wolfram Engine and run wolframscript -file calistir.wls to write example PNGs into ciktilar/. The Mathematica code passes a syntax check but hasn't been run in Mathematica yet. Every image on this page comes from the Python port.
